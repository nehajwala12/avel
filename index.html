Section 03 · Resource allocation

Clinician hours are rostered to contracted coverage, not to the hours when encounters arrive

Avel's product is reliable access to clinicians, so labour is most of its cost and the roster is its main margin lever. The exhibits on this tab are illustrative; they show what Phase 1 produces from eSync schedules, session logs, payroll and credentialing records, pool by pool and hour by hour.

Emergency demand peaks between 17:00 and 22:00, while the roster holds capacity flat from 07:00

The illustrative emergency pool below carries spare capacity through the morning and runs short every evening. The same clinician hours, staggered to arrive later, would cover the peak without adding headcount. Phase 1 builds this view for every pool from eSync schedules and encounter timestamps.

Low-volume lines carry the most idle time; psychiatry carries the most overload

Utilisation is not a single number to maximise. Below the safe band, paid capacity sits idle; above it, response times stretch. The question for each pool is how many hours sit outside the band, and at what time of day.

Premium labour has risen from 6% to more than 9% of clinical labour cost in eighteen months

Overtime and locum cover are the price of a roster that does not match demand, and of psychiatry vacancies inherited with acquired books. The locum premium runs 30–40% above employed rates in every specialty.

A handful of clinicians carry whole states for some specialties, which caps how far rosters can flex

A clinician can only cover encounters in states where they are licensed and at sites where they hold privileges. Where two or three people carry a state's psychiatry or intensivist demand, rosters cannot flex without premium labour, and a single resignation becomes a service-level event.

Acquired pools run at lower utilisation than legacy pools doing the same work, except psychiatry, which runs hotter

Five acquisitions brought their own rosters, systems and habits. eSync was introduced in May 2024, partway through the acquisition run; Phase 1 checks which pools are scheduled in it and whether shared pooling has actually happened.

Reshaping rosters releases an illustrative $1.7m net, after paying for the evening cover service levels need

The sizing nets the cost of the fix. Hours moved into the evening protect response times (tab 04); hours removed from the early morning are capacity released. Every option is replayed against the service-level guardrails before it is counted.

Caution

Idle time can be waste, or capacity the service needs. EMS and crisis lines must answer immediately at low volume, so their floor sits at 25%; low utilisation there is the product, not the problem.

Note

FTE in Exhibit 3.06 is clinical capacity. Avel's published figure of more than 600 physicians, nurses and specialists counts people, many part-time; Phase 1 reconciles the two.

Section 04 · Service levels

Median response looks healthy; the slow tail breaches in the same evening hours the roster under-covers

Service levels are the constraint on every efficiency idea and the evidence base for every renewal. This tab shows, with illustrative data, how Phase 1 measures response at the median and the tail, by service, hour and platform, and how breaches turn into revenue at risk.

The median emergency response barely moves through the day; the slow tail more than doubles in the evening

Averages reassure and tails lose contracts. A rural ED physician waiting fourteen minutes for a remote colleague remembers the wait, not the 3.8-minute median. Phase 1 measures every service at the median and the 90th percentile, by hour, from session timestamps.

Acquired platforms run a slower tail than legacy Avel workflows doing the same work

Pharmacy, hospitalist and psychiatry each arrived partly through acquisition. Where the acquired book still runs on its original platform, its P90 is slower, its connection failures higher and its documentation later.

Failed connections have risen 40% in a year, and most failures start at the customer's site

A remote clinician is only as available as the connection. Failures that start in a hospital's bandwidth or ageing endpoint are not Avel's fault, but they are Avel's service level and Avel's renewal. Phase 1 separates the causes Avel controls from those it can contract around.

Response time bends upward once utilisation passes about 85%, which sets the ceiling for every roster change

Every efficiency finding on tab 03 must pass a service test before it counts. The relationship below is what makes the test concrete: below 80% utilisation, extra load costs little response time; above 85%, it costs a lot.

Breaches turn into money at renewal: an illustrative $3.1m of annual value renews within twelve months on contracts with recent breaches

Few contracts pay explicit service credits. The cost of a missed standard usually arrives later, as a concession at renewal or a scope reduction. That is why the register reports revenue at risk on its own line and never nets it against savings.

The published evidence supports access and fewer transfers; Avel's own outcome claims need their methods attached

Customers renew on reliability and on value they can see. The public evidence base is substantive but mixed, and several company-reported figures lack the period, denominator or comparator a hospital CFO would ask for. Phase 1 can rebuild them from Avel's records with the method shown.

Caution

One contract in five costs at least 99% of its price to serve, and the bottom decile loses money

Averages hide the tail. Once shared clinician pools, supervision, technology and account support are allocated by encounter minutes, contract economics spread widely. The bottom decile is small contracts with high-acuity coverage commitments and legacy pricing.

Clinician cost is growing faster than contract escalators; scale has so far outrun the wedge

A 3.5% annual escalator, the rate in Avel's public Geneva EMS agreement, compounds to 19% over five years. An illustrative 5% clinician cost growth compounds to 28%. Margin has still risen because volume spread fixed cost. Once growth slows, the wedge shows up directly in gross margin.

An illustrative 30% of billable excess-use and after-hours events are never invoiced

Avel's contracts charge for encounters above a cap and for pharmacy coverage outside contracted hours; both policies are public. Whether every eligible event becomes an invoice is not. This is usually the fastest cash in a diagnostic, and it is shown once and dated, never annualised twice.

The APC book was bought at 1.1x trailing revenue while its revenue was declining; its margin path is the test of the price

Amwell's filings make APC unusually transparent. Revenue fell from $24.0m in 2023 to $22.7m in 2024, the year before the sale. Phase 1 tracks each acquired book's margin and retention from its own transaction date.
